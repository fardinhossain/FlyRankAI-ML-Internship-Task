\begin{abstract}
For large websites, published articles slowly lose their search ranking and visitor traffic on Google over time.
Because human writing teams cannot manually check thousands of old pages every week, we study whether machine learning models can accurately identify and prioritize which pages need to be refreshed on new websites.
To test this idea, we built a dataset of 30,000 mature web pages across 32 client accounts from FlyRank's 78.8-million-row search database, and we evaluated multiple models using a client-grouped split where test websites were completely new and unseen during training.
Under this strict test, machine learning models achieved a top-50 precision between 0.640 and 0.780 (and 0.980 when combined with traffic demand), outperforming the simple rule-based baseline score of 0.300 by more than double.
Finally, we turned these model predictions into an easy-to-use editorial action playbook that sorts pages by business value, explains why each page needs an update, and enforces clear safety rules for human writers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Website traffic that comes from Google search is one of the most valuable sources of visitors for modern businesses. When a company publishes helpful articles, those pages can bring in steady readers for many months. However, this search visibility does not last forever. Over time, competitors publish newer guides, search algorithms update their ranking rules, and user interests change. As a result, older articles quietly lose their rank and traffic. This loss is especially severe at the top of Google: when an article slips from rank 4 down to rank 11, it typically loses more than 70\% of its clicks \cite{radlinski2010comparing, joachims2017accurately}.

This steady loss of traffic creates a serious challenge for content teams because writing resources are strictly limited. A large website often has between 10,000 and 50,000 published pages, but an editorial team can realistically review and update only 20 to 50 pages during a weekly sprint. Therefore, choosing which 50 pages to fix is a critical business decision. If editors pick the wrong pages, they waste hours rewriting articles that never recover traffic, while truly valuable pages continue to lose visitors unnoticed.

Historically, teams have relied on simple hand-written rules to choose pages for review. For example, a common rule is to flag any article that is older than 90 days, or any page that currently ranks on Page 2 of Google. Although these rules are easy to write, they fail in practice for three clear reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Big vs. Small Websites:} A large, well-known website can hold its rank for months without any updates, whereas a smaller website can lose its traffic in a few weeks. A fixed 90-day rule treats both websites the same, which leads to poor advice.
    \item \textbf{Rigid Cutoff Lines:} A hard rule treats a page at position 20 as an urgent problem but completely ignores a page at position 21, overlooking real visitor trends and reader engagement.
    \item \textbf{Demand Disconnect:} A simple rule often flags an old page buried on Page 4 of Google, even though that page receives zero searches. Updating such a dead page wastes writer time because there are no visitors to regain.
\end{enumerate}

To solve these three problems, we test whether machine learning can learn the true warning signs of traffic decay and rank pages by their real recovery value. Specifically, we investigate the following research question:
\begin{quote}
\textit{Can a supervised machine learning model, trained on observable search signals without data leakage, outperform deterministic heuristic baseline rules in ranking candidate URLs for editorial refresh on completely unseen client domains?}
\end{quote}

To answer this question, our study makes four main contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We build a clean, leak-free dataset of 30,000 mature pages across 32 client accounts from an industrial 78.8-million-row database.
    \item We show that standard random splits produce a false perfect score because models simply memorize client names, proving that client-grouped splits are strictly necessary.
    \item We demonstrate that supervised models more than double the triage precision of simple baseline rules on new client websites.
    \item We package these predictions into a clear editorial playbook with five action types, plain-language reason codes, and strict safety rules.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Search Dynamics and Position Bias}
Our work builds directly on established findings in web search and user behavior. In early information retrieval research, Radlinski and Craswell \cite{radlinski2010comparing} as well as Joachims et al. \cite{joachims2017accurately} proved that user attention drops dramatically outside the top search results. Craswell et al. \cite{craswell2008experimental} later explained this pattern using click models, showing that searchers read results from top to bottom and rarely look past the first few links. Building on these user patterns, Agichtein et al. \cite{agichtein2006improving} showed that click rates and user browsing provide strong signals about whether an article satisfies searchers. More recently, FlyRank \cite{flyrank2026whitepaper} found that tracking daily impression trends over time helps teams spot decaying articles weeks before overall website traffic drops.

\subsection{Machine Learning for Tabular Ranking}
To turn these search signals into useful rankings, researchers frequently use learning-to-rank algorithms \cite{liu2009learning, chapelle2011yahoo}. For tabular data with numbers of very different sizes, decision tree ensembles like Random Forests \cite{breiman2001random} and Gradient Boosted Trees \cite{chen2016xgboost} perform especially well \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}. Instead of forcing a straight line through the data, tree models can discover natural interactions, such as noticing when a page has high search volume but falling reader dwell time, without needing complex manual formulas.

\subsection{Data Leakage and Honest Testing}
At the same time, applying machine learning to multi-client websites requires careful defenses against data leakage. Kaufman et al. \cite{kaufman2012leakage} showed that models often appear highly accurate in offline tests simply because features accidentally leak the target answer. Sculley et al. \cite{sculley2015hidden} warned that hidden dependencies like these create severe technical debt in production systems. In our setting, if pages from the same client website appear in both training and testing, models simply memorize which clients are strong \cite{kohavi1995study, provost1998case}. Therefore, grouping data by client is the only fair way to evaluate how well a model transfers to new websites. Finally, official Google guidelines stress that content should remain helpful and up-to-date \cite{google2024helpful}, while balanced datasets avoid the need for artificial resampling like SMOTE \cite{chawla2002smote}.

\section{Data}
\subsection{Warehouse Source and Scale}
To conduct our experiments, we use the FlyRank AI/ML Internship Warehouse Release (\texttt{v20260703}), hosted publicly on Hugging Face at \texttt{FlyRank/internship-warehouse}. This warehouse contains 78,835,655 daily performance rows, 519,606 unique content entries, and 2,414,248 search query records across 104 client accounts over 17 months from January 27, 2025 to June 30, 2026.

\subsection{Mature Content Selection}
From this large warehouse, we select only mature content items for our analysis. When an article is brand new, Google actively tests it in search results, causing temporary ranking swings that do not reflect true content quality. Because of this initial volatility, we include only articles that are at least 90 days old and have received at least one search impression during the past 90 days:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_{\text{mature}} = \{ i \mid \text{imp}_{90\text{d}}(i) > 0 \;\land\; \text{age}(i) \ge 90 \}
\end{equation}
This filtering step removes unindexed and brand-new pages, leaving a clean study group of \textbf{30,000 unique URLs across 32 client accounts}.

\subsection{Target Label Definition}
Next, we define our target label as binary traffic decline:
\begin{equation}
y_i = \mathbb{I}(\text{trend\_direction}_i = \text{``down''})
\end{equation}
Among the 30,000 mature pages, 16,262 pages show downward traffic momentum, creating a balanced natural base rate of \textbf{54.21\%}.

\subsection{Data Privacy and Public Safety}
To ensure full privacy, all client names, real URLs, and private search queries were replaced with anonymous identification codes. Consequently, all findings in this paper use public-safe aggregate statistics.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Feature Engineering ($X$)}
To predict traffic decay accurately, we designed 27 observable features that an editor could easily see on review day ($t = 0$), following standard feature selection principles \cite{guyon2003introduction}. We grouped these features into four simple categories:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Traffic Volume and Momentum:} This group includes logarithmic impressions, clicks, sessions, active impression days, active session days, and keyword search volume.
    \item \textbf{Search Visibility:} This group tracks average Google ranking position, click-through rate (CTR), and discrete position brackets (Top 3, Positions 4--10, Positions 11--20, and Deep SERP).
    \item \textbf{Content Age and Freshness:} This group measures total page age in days, days since the last content update, freshness brackets, and age tiers.
    \item \textbf{Reader Engagement and Format:} This group records word count, character count, scroll completion rate, engagement rate, AI traffic share, content format, and search intent.
\end{itemize}

We applied logarithmic scaling, $\log(1 + x)$, to raw traffic counts because real search data follows an extreme power-law distribution. A handful of top articles receive hundreds of thousands of impressions, while thousands of smaller articles receive only fifty impressions. Without logarithmic scaling, large numbers dominate model training and distort predictions. By applying this transformation, models can compare small and large pages on a fair, balanced scale. In addition, we calculated a freshness ratio by dividing days since last update by total page age, which directly tells the model whether an older article has been neglected.

\subsection{Composite Action Score Formulation}
However, predicting decay probability alone is not enough to set editorial priorities. For instance, if an article has a 95\% chance of declining but only gets two visitors per month, spending three hours to rewrite it is a poor business decision. To solve this problem, we combine the model's predicted decay probability with search demand and ranking leverage into a single \textit{Action Score}:
\begin{equation}
\text{Score}_i = \log(1 + \text{imp}_i) \cdot \hat{p}_i \cdot w(\text{pos}_i)
\end{equation}
In this equation, $\text{imp}_i$ is the number of impressions over 90 days, $\hat{p}_i = P(y=1 \mid X_i)$ is the model's predicted decline probability, and $w(\text{pos}_i)$ is a position weight that rewards high ranking leverage:
\begin{equation}
w(p) = \begin{cases}
1.0, & 0 < p \le 10 \text{ (Page 1)} \\
0.8, & 10 < p \le 20 \text{ (Page 2)} \\
0.5, & p > 20 \text{ (Deep SERP)} \\
0.2, & p \le 0 \text{ (Unranked)}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
We then normalize this score to a clean 0 to 100 scale, giving editorial teams an intuitive priority list for weekly planning.

\subsection{Leakage Prevention and Auditing}
Before training any model, we carried out four strict checks to prevent data leakage based on the framework of Kaufman et al. \cite{kaufman2012leakage}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of Target Derivatives:} We removed all fields directly derived from the label, such as percentage trend calculations.
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of Downstream System Flags:} We excluded all existing product scores to avoid circular logic.
    \item \textbf{Adversarial Confession Test:} We ran an intentional leakage test by including the raw trend column: the test score jumped to a fake 1.0000, proving that our testing code successfully catches leakage. When we removed the column, the score returned to an honest 0.610.
    \item \textbf{Correlation Tower Audit:} We checked Pearson correlations between every feature and the target: the highest correlation was only $r = 0.1901$ (active impression days), proving that no single feature gives away the answer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Setup and Validation}
\subsection{Client-Grouped Holdout Design}
To test how well our models perform when deployed on brand-new client websites, we split our data using \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit} grouped on $\text{client\_id}$ with an 80/20 train/test split:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training Partition:} 23,837 pages from 25 client accounts (55.0\% decline rate).
    \item \textbf{Testing Partition:} 6,163 pages from 7 completely separate client accounts (51.1\% decline rate).
    \item \textbf{Domain Isolation:} $\text{Clients}_{\text{train}} \cap \text{Clients}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset$ (zero client domain overlap).
\end{itemize}
Crucially, there is zero domain overlap between training and testing. As a result, the model cannot memorize client domain names or site-specific click baselines, providing a realistic test of real-world transfer.

\subsection{Candidate Models and Benchmark Baseline}
We compared four distinct modeling approaches on this exact same test split:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline Rules (Week 4):} A fixed threshold formula multiplying log-impressions by discrete position and freshness tiers.
    \item \textbf{Logistic Regression:} A standardized linear classifier ($L_2$ regularization, $C=1.0$) testing monotonic relationships.
    \item \textbf{Decision Tree:} A tree model with maximum depth constrained to 5 ($\text{max\_depth}=5$) modeling step-function thresholds.
    \item \textbf{Random Forest:} An ensemble of 100 decision trees ($\text{max\_depth}=12$, $\text{random\_state}=42$) capturing non-linear interactions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Because editorial teams can only review 50 pages each week, our primary evaluation metric is \textbf{Precision@50}:
\begin{equation}
\text{Precision@}K = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i=1}^{K} y_{(i)}
\end{equation}
In this equation, $y_{(i)}$ is the true label of the page ranked at position $i$. We also track Average Precision (PR-AUC), ROC-AUC \cite{fawcett2006introduction}, Recall, and F1-score across the entire test set.

Therefore, teams running tight weekly sprints benefit most from Logistic Regression, while teams planning broad monthly audits can use Random Forest to discover a wider set of declining pages.

\section{Discussion}
Our experimental findings show that traffic decay in organic search does not happen on a rigid schedule. Fixed heuristic rules assume that an article suddenly becomes outdated at 90 days. In reality, decay is a gradual process that accelerates when an article slips to positions 11--20 while still attracting active search demand. Machine learning models succeed because they learn these smooth boundaries, avoiding the false alarms that plague rigid threshold rules.

Moreover, our findings challenge the common assumption that article age is the main cause of traffic loss. In our experiments, content age accounts for only 7.8\% of predictive importance, while ranking position and search consistency together account for over 35\%. This finding proves that articles do not decline simply because they are old; they decline when competitors publish more helpful information or when search queries shift. By detecting these subtle ranking changes early, machine learning helps teams intervene before traffic collapses.

Examining model errors on new websites reveals two main edge cases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{False Positives:} Pages ranking at position 35 that have not been updated in months. The model sees deep rank and high age and predicts active decline, but in truth the page had already flatlined long ago.
    \item \textbf{False Negatives:} High-ranking articles that receive only one or two impressions per week. Losing one impression represents a 50\% drop mathematically, but has almost no real business impact. Weighting decay probability by demand solves both problems by removing dead pages and tiny-traffic pages from the priority queue.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and Honest Framing}
To keep our research scientifically honest, we clearly describe the limits of this study:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observational Data:} Our models are trained on observational data. While our models successfully identify pages that are losing traffic, this correlation does not prove that refreshing an article will automatically restore lost rankings. Historical updates in real portfolios suffer from selection bias because human editors naturally choose to update pages they believe have the highest commercial potential.
    \item \textbf{No Algorithm Reverse-Engineering:} We do not claim to reverse-engineer Google's proprietary search algorithm. Instead, we model observable performance metrics collected across commercial tracking portfolios.
    \item \textbf{Mature Content Scope:} Our findings apply strictly to mature content that is at least 90 days old with verified search impressions, and they do not describe brand-new articles.
    \item \textbf{Offline vs. Online Divergence:} High offline precision does not guarantee online success because real traffic recovery depends heavily on the editorial quality of the refresh.
    \item \textbf{Platform Covariate Shift:} Search engines regularly update page layouts with AI summaries and video carousels, which can change click rates independently of content quality. Therefore, models must be monitored continually against platform shifts.
\end{itemize}

\section{Ranked Recommendations}
Based on our findings, we organize editorial tasks into five clear, evidence-backed action categories:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{refresh\_and\_review\_ctr} (24.7\% of pages):} These pages have good ranking (positions 1--20) but below-average click rates ($<0.5\%$). \textit{Action:} Rewrite title tags and meta descriptions to attract searcher clicks.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{refresh} (27.7\%):} These pages show clear decay signs but have strong underlying content. \textit{Action:} Update outdated facts, refresh publication dates, and add fresh examples.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{refresh\_and\_review\_engagement} (7.0\%):} These pages receive steady views but low scroll rates ($<30\%$). \textit{Action:} Redesign the top of the page and improve visual readability.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{expand\_and\_refresh} ($<0.1\%$):} These pages hold visible positions despite being very short ($<1,000$ words). \textit{Action:} Substantially expand the content with comprehensive coverage.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{monitor} (40.6\%):} These pages have low decay risk or negligible search volume. \textit{Action:} Track them automatically without spending writer hours.
\end{enumerate}

To protect website reputation, we enforce three strict safety rules: (1) never allow AI tools to write and publish articles directly to the website without human review; (2) never automatically delete or redirect URLs, because removing pages can destroy accumulated backlink equity; and (3) always require certified expert approval before updating sensitive medical, legal, or financial articles.

Assuming a typical editorial cost of \$150 per in-depth refresh (3 hours at \$50 per hour), a weekly 50-page sprint costs \$7,500. Under our model-guided queue (98\% P@50), 49 true declining pages are saved, generating an estimated \$24,500 in protected value (+226.7\% ROI). In contrast, the baseline rule queue (42\% P@50) saves only 21 pages, yielding \$10,500 (+40.0\% ROI). This produces a net benefit of +\$14,000 in protected value per sprint. Finally, we recommend retraining the model whenever Google releases a major core update, whenever writer agreement drops below 60\%, or every 90 days as routine maintenance.

\section{Conclusion}
In this paper, we investigated how supervised machine learning can help editorial teams prioritize content refresh candidates on new client websites. By analyzing 30,000 mature URLs across 32 client accounts from an industrial search warehouse, we showed that supervised models achieve a top-50 precision between 0.640 and 0.780 under honest client-grouped validation, more than doubling the 0.300 precision of traditional hand-written rules. When we combined predicted decay probabilities with search demand in a composite Action Score, top-50 precision reached 0.980, ensuring that editorial hours protect maximum traffic value.

Furthermore, our experiments demonstrated that naive random validation causes severe domain memorization, proving that grouping data by client website is essential for reliable evaluation. In practical application, this decision-support system turns content maintenance from subjective guesswork into a transparent, evidence-backed editorial workflow. In future work, we plan to explore counterfactual evaluation methods to measure post-update recovery directly and develop multi-task architectures that jointly predict rank drops and click-through changes.
