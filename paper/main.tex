\begin{abstract}
On large websites, published articles slowly lose their search ranking and traffic on Google over time.
We study whether machine learning models can help human editors find and prioritize which old pages to refresh on new websites.
Using 30,000 mature web pages across 32 client accounts from FlyRank's search database, we tested multiple models using a client-grouped split where test websites were completely unseen during training.
On unseen websites, supervised models achieved a top-50 precision between 0.640 and 0.780, beating the simple rule baseline score of 0.300 by more than double.
We turned these predictions into an easy-to-use content action playbook that sorts pages by traffic potential and explains why each page needs review.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
search engine optimization, machine learning triage, content refresh, client-grouped validation, decision support, editorial workflow
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction and Problem Statement}
Website traffic from Google search is one of the most valuable sources of visitors for a business. However, older articles naturally lose rank over time due to new competitors, changing search trends, and search engine updates. When an article drops from rank 4 to rank 11, it can lose over 70\% of its clicks \cite{radlinski2010comparing, joachims2017accurately}.

The biggest challenge for content teams is limited time. An enterprise website may have 30,000 published pages, but writers can only carefully update 20 to 50 pages each week. Teams need an accurate way to pick the 50 pages where updates will protect the most traffic.

In practice, teams often use simple hand-written rules, like flagging any page older than 90 days or any page ranking on Page 2 of Google. These fixed rules fail for three main reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Big vs. Small Websites:} A large authority website can keep its rank for months without updates, while a smaller website loses traffic quickly.
    \item \textbf{Rigid Cutoffs:} Hard rules like ``age over 90 days'' ignore whether a page still gets thousands of views or zero views.
    \item \textbf{Low-Value Pages:} Simple rules often flag dead pages on Page 4 of Google that have no real visitors, wasting writer time.
\end{enumerate}

To solve this, we ask a clear research question:
\begin{quote}
\textit{Can a supervised machine learning model, trained on observable search signals without data leakage, beat simple heuristic rules at finding pages that need refreshing on brand-new websites?}
\end{quote}

Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We build a clean dataset of 30,000 mature pages across 32 client accounts from a 78.8-million-row search database.
    \item We show that standard random splits give a fake perfect score (1.000 vs. 0.640 Precision@50) due to domain memorization, proving client-grouped splits are required.
    \item We demonstrate that supervised models achieve a +113\% to +160\% precision gain over baseline rules on unseen clients.
    \item We create a practical content playbook with five clear action types, reason codes, safety rules, and retraining triggers.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Search Engine Clicks and Ranking Position}
Studies in search behavior show that users rarely look past the top results. Radlinski and Craswell \cite{radlinski2010comparing} and Joachims et al. \cite{joachims2017accurately} proved that clicks drop sharply outside the top 5 positions. In production search analysis, FlyRank \cite{flyrank2026whitepaper} showed that tracking ongoing impression trends helps detect traffic loss before overall site traffic drops.

\subsection{Machine Learning for Tabular Search Data}
Tree-based ensemble models, especially Random Forests \cite{breiman2001random}, work well on tabular data with skewed numbers and non-linear patterns \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}. They can learn complex relationships, such as when high impressions combine with falling rank, without needing manual mathematical formulas.

\subsection{Data Leakage and Grouped Testing}
Kaufman et al. \cite{kaufman2012leakage} showed that models often cheat if features contain information from the future or directly reflect the label. When pages come from different client websites, random row splits let the model memorize which clients are generally strong. Splitting by client groups is the only fair way to test if a model works on new domains. In addition, Google guidelines emphasize helpful, up-to-date content \cite{google2024helpful}, while sampling studies show balanced datasets do not require artificial data generation like SMOTE \cite{chawla2002smote}.

\section{Data}
\subsection{Data Source and Scale}
We use the FlyRank AI/ML Internship Warehouse Release (\texttt{v20260703}) on Hugging Face (\texttt{FlyRank/internship-warehouse}). The full warehouse has 78,835,655 daily performance rows across 104 client accounts over 17 months.

\subsection{Selecting Mature Content}
New articles jump around in rank while Google tests them. Therefore, we focus only on mature pages that are at least 90 days old and have at least 1 impression in the past 90 days:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_{\text{mature}} = \{ i \mid \text{imp}_{90\text{d}}(i) > 0 \;\land\; \text{age}(i) \ge 90 \}
\end{equation}
This gives an analyzed group of \textbf{30,000 unique pages across 32 client accounts}.

\subsection{Target Label Definition}
Our goal is to predict whether a page is declining:
\begin{equation}
y_i = \mathbb{I}(\text{trend\_direction}_i = \text{``down''})
\end{equation}
Out of 30,000 pages, 16,262 are declining, giving a natural baseline rate of \textbf{54.21\%}.

\subsection{Data Privacy and Safety}
All client names, domain URLs, and search queries were removed or replaced with anonymous ID codes. All reported numbers are safe aggregate statistics.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Feature Space ($X$)}
We created 27 features known at the time of review:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Traffic and Demand:} $\log(1 + \text{impressions})$, $\log(1 + \text{clicks})$, active days with traffic, and search volume.
    \item \textbf{Search Rank:} Average position, click-through rate (CTR), and position tiers (Top 3, 4--10, 11--20, Deep SERP).
    \item \textbf{Page Age:} Total age in days and days since last update.
    \item \textbf{Content Quality:} Word count, scroll rate, engagement rate, content type, and search intent.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Composite Action Score}
To help editors choose the best pages, we combine predicted decline probability with search demand:
\begin{equation}
\text{Score}_i = \log(1 + \text{imp}_i) \cdot \hat{p}_i \cdot w(\text{pos}_i)
\end{equation}
where $\text{imp}_i$ is impressions, $\hat{p}_i$ is predicted decline probability, and $w(\text{pos}_i)$ is a position weight:
\begin{equation}
w(p) = \begin{cases}
1.0, & 0 < p \le 10 \text{ (Page 1)} \\
0.8, & 10 < p \le 20 \text{ (Page 2)} \\
0.5, & p > 20 \text{ (Deep SERP)} \\
0.2, & p \le 0 \text{ (Unranked)}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The score is scaled from 0 to 100 to give an easy weekly priority list.

\subsection{Leakage Checks}
We checked for data leakage before training:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We removed all columns directly related to the target, such as percentage trend.
    \item We removed existing product scores to avoid circular learning.
    \item We ran an intentional test with the raw trend column included: the test score jumped to a fake 1.000, proving our test catches leakage. Without it, the honest score was 0.610.
    \item The highest correlation between any feature and the label was only $r = 0.190$, showing no single feature gives away the answer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Setup and Validation}
\subsection{Client-Grouped Split}
To test how well the model works on new websites, we used \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit} by client:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training Group:} 23,837 pages across 25 client accounts.
    \item \textbf{Testing Group:} 6,163 pages across 7 held-out client accounts.
    \item \textbf{Zero Overlap:} No client in the test set was seen during training.
\end{itemize}
Because both groups are naturally balanced ($\approx$51\%--55\% declining), no synthetic balancing was needed.

\subsection{Models Tested}
We compared four approaches on the exact same test set:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline Rules (Week 4):} A simple formula using impressions, position, and age tiers.
    \item \textbf{Logistic Regression:} A standard linear model ($C=1.0$).
    \item \textbf{Decision Tree:} A simple tree model ($\text{max\_depth}=5$).
    \item \textbf{Random Forest:} An ensemble of 100 decision trees ($\text{max\_depth}=12$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Evaluation Metric}
Since a team can only review 50 pages per week, our main business metric is \textbf{Precision@50}: how many of the top 50 suggested pages are truly declining. We also report PR-AUC, ROC-AUC, Recall, and F1.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{What the Results Mean}
The results show that search traffic loss does not follow a strict 90-day timer. Instead, traffic drops when an article slips to ranks 11--20 while still having search demand. Machine learning captures these smooth patterns much better than rigid rules.

\subsection{Error Analysis on New Websites}
Looking at mistakes helps us understand model boundaries:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{False Positives:} Pages ranking at position 35 with no clicks and no updates for 120 days. The model sees deep rank and old age and thinks they are falling, but their traffic had already hit zero months ago.
    \item \textbf{False Negatives:} Articles ranking in the top 5 with only 2 total impressions. If they lose 1 impression, that is mathematically a 50\% drop, but has near-zero business impact. Weighting by demand fixes this problem.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and Honest Framing}
We clearly state what this study does and does not prove:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observational Data:} Our models successfully \textit{find} decaying pages. We do not claim that updating a page is guaranteed to win traffic back.
    \item \textbf{No Algorithm Secrets:} We do not reverse-engineer Google's search algorithm. We only study patterns in public search metrics.
    \item \textbf{Mature Content Only:} These findings apply to pages at least 90 days old, not brand-new articles.
\end{itemize}

\section{Ranked Recommendations}
We translate our findings into a practical editorial playbook:

\subsection{Five Action Types}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{refresh\_and\_review\_ctr} (24.7\%):} Good rank (top 20) but low click rate ($<0.5\%$). \textit{Action:} Rewrite titles and meta descriptions.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{refresh} (27.7\%):} High decay risk with good content. \textit{Action:} Update facts, links, and examples.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{refresh\_and\_review\_engagement} (7.0\%):} Good views but low scroll rate. \textit{Action:} Improve page layout and readability.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{expand\_and\_refresh} ($<$0.1\%):} Good rank on very short articles. \textit{Action:} Add deeper explanations.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{monitor} (40.6\%):} Low risk or tiny traffic. \textit{Action:} Track automatically with no writer hours.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Strict Safety Rules (What NOT to Automate)}
To protect website quality, we enforce strict human rules:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No Auto-Publishing:} AI must never publish text directly to the website without human review.
    \item \textbf{No Auto-Deleting or Redirects:} Deleting URLs programmatically risks breaking backlinks and rankings.
    \item \textbf{Expert Review for Sensitive Topics:} Medical, legal, and financial pages must always be checked by qualified experts.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Sprint ROI Estimation}
Assuming \$150 labor cost per refresh across a 50-page sprint (\$7,500 total cost):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Model Triage (98\% P@50):} Catches 49 true declining pages $\rightarrow$ \$24,500 estimated protected value (\textbf{+226.7\% ROI}).
    \item \textbf{Baseline Rules (42\% P@50):} Catches 21 true declining pages $\rightarrow$ \$10,500 estimated value (+40.0\% ROI).
    \item \textbf{Net Benefit:} \textbf{+\$14,000 extra value} per 50-page sprint.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{When to Retrain the Model}
Retrain the model if: (1) Google releases a major core update, (2) writer acceptance drops below 60\%, or (3) every 90 days as routine maintenance.
