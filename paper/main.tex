\begin{abstract}
In large-scale organic search, published content undergoes continuous, silent traffic decay that human editorial teams cannot manually triage across tens of thousands of URLs.
We investigate whether supervised machine learning classifiers trained on observable search performance signals can prioritize content refresh candidates more effectively than deterministic heuristic rules on newly onboarded client domains.
Using an audited 30,000-page mature content slice derived from FlyRank's 78.8-million-record warehouse release across 32 client domains, we engineered 27 observable search features under a strictly isolated client-grouped split with zero domain overlap.
On unseen client domains, supervised models achieved a measured Precision@50 of 0.640 to 0.780 (compared to the 0.511 holdout base rate), delivering a +113\% to +160\% relative precision lift over the deterministic Week-4 baseline heuristic (0.300).
We operationalized these predictions into an evidence-backed editorial action playbook that scales decay probability by search demand volume, routes URLs to five inspectable action categories with explainable reason codes, and establishes human-in-the-loop governance guardrails.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Problem Statement}
In enterprise organic search intelligence, published content represents a compounding yet continuously decaying capital asset. Once indexed, articles face competitive ranking pressure, evolving search intent, and algorithmic core updates. This decay process is frequently silent: a Page 1 URL slipping from position 4 to position 11 sheds over 70\% of its organic click-through rate (CTR) before traditional analytics dashboards register noticeable site-wide traffic drops \cite{radlinski2010comparing, joachims2017accurately}.

The central operational bottleneck in large-scale content publishing is \textit{editorial bandwidth allocation}. While an enterprise website often maintains between 10,000 and 50,000 indexed URLs, a human editorial sprint can realistically audit, verify, and refresh only 20 to 50 URLs per week. Deciding which 50 candidate pages to audit first constitutes a high-stakes ranking challenge where poor triage incurs steep opportunity costs.

Historically, content operations rely on hand-written heuristic rules, such as flagging pages where content age exceeds 90 days or where average position resides between 11 and 20. However, fixed heuristic rules exhibit three core structural weaknesses:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Domain Authority Confounding:} Fixed staleness cutoffs perform inconsistently across client domains. High-authority enterprise sites retain rank stability despite 200 days of staleness, whereas niche websites experience rapid decay.
    \item \textbf{Arbitrary Step-Function Cliffs:} Hard thresholds (e.g., position $\le 20$) discard rich non-linear interactions across impressions, scroll engagement, and search velocity.
    \item \textbf{Demand Disconnect:} Pure decay indicators frequently flag dormant deep-SERP URLs (position 40) that command zero real-world commercial value, wasting reviewer hours.
\end{enumerate}

To address these challenges, we formulate the refresh triage challenge as a supervised classification and demand-weighted ranking task. We investigate the following formal research question:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Can a supervised machine learning classifier, trained on observable multi-signal search features without target leakage, outperform deterministic heuristic baseline rules in ranking candidate URLs for editorial refresh on completely unseen client domains?}
\end{quote}

Our primary contributions are fourfold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We define a leak-free data contract on 30,000 mature URLs across 32 client accounts derived from a 78.8-million-row production search warehouse.
    \item We demonstrate that standard random row splits produce artificial domain memorization (1.000 vs. 0.640 Precision@50), establishing the necessity of client-grouped validation.
    \item We evaluate Logistic Regression, Decision Trees, and Random Forests against a deterministic heuristic baseline, proving a +113\% to +160\% relative precision lift on unseen domains.
    \item We operationalize predictions into an actionable content playbook featuring five intervention archetypes, transparent reason codes, a strict ``no-go'' automation guardrail, and retrain triggers.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Longitudinal Search Performance and Rank Dynamics}
Search engine result page (SERP) stability has been widely studied in information retrieval. Radlinski and Craswell \cite{radlinski2010comparing} demonstrated that organic user attention exhibits a steep position bias cliff, where positions outside the top 5 suffer catastrophic CTR decay. Joachims et al. \cite{joachims2017accurately} further modeled how implicit click-through signals reflect relative position rather than absolute document relevance. In industrial content operations, the FlyRank AI Research Group \cite{flyrank2026whitepaper} observed that trailing search velocity and impression continuity serve as robust empirical indicators of post-index rank stabilization.

\subsection{Supervised Ranking and Ensemble Methods}
Supervised tree-based ensembles, particularly Random Forests \cite{breiman2001random}, have proven resilient in tabular domains characterized by correlated continuous features, skewed marginal distributions, and non-linear interactions \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}. Unlike single-tree thresholding or linear models, random forests naturally capture multi-signal interactions (e.g., high impressions coupled with declining scroll depth) without hand-crafted polynomial terms.

\subsection{Data Leakage and Grouped Validation in Domain Systems}
Kaufman et al. \cite{kaufman2012leakage} established a formal taxonomy of data leakage in commercial data mining, categorizing leakage into label-derived features, temporal window overlap, and decision-derived product flags. In domain-structured settings where URLs cluster by client domain, random partitioning allows models to memorize entity-specific baselines. Grouped cross-validation (holding out entire entity clusters) is recognized as the only valid offline design for evaluating domain transfer generalizability.

\section{Data}
\subsection{Warehouse Source and Scale}
This study utilizes the FlyRank AI/ML Internship Warehouse Release (\texttt{v20260703}), hosted on Hugging Face at \texttt{FlyRank/internship-warehouse}. The full warehouse records 78,835,655 daily performance rows, 519,606 content metadata dimensions, and 2,414,248 query-level entries across 104 client tracking portfolios over 17 months (Jan. 27, 2025 to June 30, 2026).

\subsection{Mature Content Slice Selection}
In accordance with our data contract, we constrain experimental analysis to the \textit{mature content slice}:
\begin{equation}
\text{Mature Population} = \{ i \mid \text{impressions}_{90\text{d}}(i) > 0 \land \text{age\_days}(i) \ge 90 \}
\end{equation}
Articles younger than 90 days are deliberately excluded: newly launched URLs undergo search engine crawling discovery and algorithmic sandbox testing, yielding high ranking volatility that does not reflect editorial obsolescence. The resulting analyzed population comprises \textbf{30,000 unique URLs across 32 client accounts}.

\subsection{Target Label Definition}
The ground-truth classification target is binary content performance decay:
\begin{equation}
y_i = \mathbb{I}(\text{trend\_direction}_i = \text{``down''})
\end{equation}
Across the 30,000 mature content items, 16,262 items exhibit negative momentum, yielding a natural population base rate of \textbf{0.5421} (54.21\%).

\subsection{Public Safety and Anonymization}
In compliance with research privacy protocols, all client names, domains, raw URL paths, and unhashed query strings are strictly excluded or replaced with cryptographic pseudonyms (\texttt{content\_id}, \texttt{client\_id}). All experimental outputs report public-safe aggregate statistics.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Feature Space Formulation ($X$)}
We engineered 27 observable features knowable at triage time ($t = 0$), spanning four distinct diagnostic dimensions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Search Volume and Momentum:} $\log(1 + \text{impressions}_{90\text{d}})$, $\log(1 + \text{clicks}_{90\text{d}})$, $\log(1 + \text{sessions}_{90\text{d}})$, $\text{days\_with\_impressions}$, $\text{days\_with\_sessions}$, search volume, and CPC.
    \item \textbf{SERP Visibility:} $\text{avg\_position}$, CTR, and position tier cutoffs (Top 3, Pos 4--10, Pos 11--20, Deep SERP).
    \item \textbf{Content Maturity and Staleness:} $\text{content\_age\_days}$, $\text{days\_since\_last\_update}$, freshness tiers, and age tiers.
    \item \textbf{Engagement and Structure:} $\text{word\_count}$, $\text{char\_count}$, $\text{engagement\_rate}$, $\text{scroll\_rate}$, $\text{ai\_traffic\_pct}$, content type, and search intent (aligning with Google Search Central guidelines on helpful content \cite{google2024helpful}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Composite Action Score Formulation}
To bridge probability estimation and editorial capacity allocation, we define a composite \textit{Action Score} that scales predicted decay probability $\hat{p}_i = P(y=1 \mid X_i)$ by search demand volume and SERP visibility:
\begin{equation}
\text{Score}_i = \log(1 + \text{impressions}_{90\text{d}, i}) \times \hat{p}_i \times w(\text{avg\_position}_i)
\end{equation}
where the position weight function $w(p)$ reflects organic search leverage:
\begin{equation}
w(p) = \begin{cases}
1.0, & \text{if } 0 < p \le 10 \text{ (Page 1)} \\
0.8, & \text{if } 10 < p \le 20 \text{ (Page 2)} \\
0.5, & \text{if } p > 20 \text{ (Deep SERP)} \\
0.2, & \text{if } p \le 0 \text{ (Unranked)}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The resulting score is normalized to a $0\text{--}100$ scale to provide a transparent, rank-ordered sprint triage queue.

\subsection{Leakage Prevention and Confession Testing}
Following Kaufman et al. \cite{kaufman2012leakage}, we audited candidate features for target leakage:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Label Derivative Exclusion:} In the source warehouse, $\text{is\_declining\_label}$ is derived from $\text{trend\_direction}$, which is calculated from $\text{trend\_pct}$. Both columns are strictly excluded from $X$.
    \item \textbf{Decision Flag Exclusion:} Production heuristic flags (e.g., $\text{baseline\_action\_score}$, $\text{health\_score}$) are excluded to prevent circular learning.
    \item \textbf{Adversarial Confession Test:} To verify test harness sensitivity, we intentionally trained a model with $\text{trend\_pct}$ included: ROC-AUC jumped to 1.0000. When removed, ROC-AUC returned to an honest 0.610.
    \item \textbf{Feature Tower Audit:} The maximum Pearson correlation between any feature and the label is $r = 0.1901$ ($\text{days\_with\_impressions}$), confirming the absence of dominant leakage towers.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Setup and Validation}
\subsection{Client-Grouped Holdout Design}
To simulate deployment on newly onboarded client domains, we implemented a grouped split using \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit} on $\text{client\_id}$ with an 80/20 train/test ratio:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training Partition:} 23,837 mature URLs across 25 client domains (Decline base rate: 0.550).
    \item \textbf{Testing Partition:} 6,163 mature URLs across 7 client domains (Decline base rate: 0.511).
    \item \textbf{Domain Isolation:} $\text{Clients}_{\text{train}} \cap \text{Clients}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset$ (Strict zero client overlap).
\end{itemize}
Given the balanced target distribution across partitions (0.550 train vs. 0.511 test), synthetic oversampling techniques such as SMOTE \cite{chawla2002smote} were not required.

\subsection{Candidate Models and Benchmark Baseline}
We benchmarked four distinct modeling paradigms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Deterministic Baseline Rule (Week 4 Heuristic):} A composite threshold rule multiplying log-impressions by discrete position and staleness tiers.
    \item \textbf{Logistic Regression:} A standardized linear classifier ($L_2$ penalty, $C=1.0$) testing monotonic relationships.
    \item \textbf{Decision Tree:} A depth-constrained classifier ($\text{max\_depth}=5$) modeling step-function thresholds.
    \item \textbf{Random Forest:} An ensemble of 100 decision trees ($\text{max\_depth}=12$, $\text{random\_state}=42$) capturing non-linear interactions without overfitting.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Because human editorial bandwidth is constrained to 50 URLs per weekly sprint, our primary operational evaluation metric is \textbf{Precision@50}:
\begin{equation}
\text{Precision@}K = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i=1}^{K} y_{(i)}
\end{equation}
where $y_{(i)}$ is the true binary label of the item ranked at position $i$. We also report Average Precision (PR-AUC), ROC-AUC, Recall, and F1-score on the held-out test partition.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Physical Mechanisms of Search Decay}
The empirical results demonstrate that search performance decay is governed by continuous non-linear thresholds rather than discrete date cutoffs. While heuristic rules assume a rigid cliff at 90 days, the learned tree structures model gradual vulnerability that accelerates when combined with deep SERP positioning (positions 15--25) and declining presence consistency.

\subsection{Error Analysis on Unseen Domains}
Examining concrete misclassifications on holdout clients illuminates model boundaries:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{False Positives ($\hat{p} \approx 0.89$, Actual $y=0$):} Stagnant deep-SERP URLs (positions 30--40) with zero clicks and updates $>100$ days ago. To the model, deep position plus high staleness fits the signature of severe decay. In reality, traffic had already flatlined, making trend velocity technically stable.
    \item \textbf{False Negatives ($\hat{p} \approx 0.10$, Actual $y=1$):} Long-form articles (3,500+ words) holding top-10 positions with micro-demand (1--2 impressions). Losing 1 impression mathematically triggers a 100\% downward trend, but carries near-zero business impact. Multiplying probability by demand volume successfully filters these items out of triage.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and Honest Framing}
In accordance with established research standards, we declare the boundaries of our study:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-Causal Observational Data:} This research proves that machine learning models effectively \textit{prioritize} decaying URLs. It does not establish that refreshing an article causes organic traffic recovery. Historical updates in observational panels suffer from editorial selection bias (human editors selectively update pages with high commercial potential).
    \item \textbf{No Algorithm Reverse-Engineering:} We do not claim to model Google's proprietary search algorithm. We model observable time-series metrics across 32 client tracking environments.
    \item \textbf{Mature Content Scope:} The findings apply strictly to content $\ge 90$ days old and do not generalize to newly launched content experiencing crawl volatility.
\end{itemize}

\section{Ranked Recommendations}
We translate our empirical findings into an operational content action playbook:

\subsection{Action Archetype Routing}
Candidate URLs are routed to five distinct interventions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{refresh\_and\_review\_ctr} (24.7\%):} Visible rank (pos $\le 20$) with below-average CTR ($< 0.5\%$). \textit{Action:} Overhaul title tags, meta descriptions, and SERP rich snippets.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{refresh} (27.7\%):} High decay probability with solid baseline content. \textit{Action:} Update factual claims, refresh dates, and add current examples.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{refresh\_and\_review\_engagement} (7.0\%):} Stable impressions but sub-30\% scroll or dwell engagement. \textit{Action:} Above-the-fold layout redesign and UX audit.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{expand\_and\_refresh} ($<$0.1\%):} Visible rank on thin content ($< 1,000$ words). \textit{Action:} Substantive topical expansion.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{monitor} (40.6\%):} Low decay risk or negligible demand. \textit{Action:} Automated tracking without human hours.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Strict ``No-Go'' List}
To prevent catastrophic operational failures, we enforce strict human-in-the-loop governance:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No Autonomous AI Publishing:} Machine learning must never auto-generate and publish copy directly to a production CMS without human editorial verification.
    \item \textbf{No Automated 301 Redirects / Pruning:} Programmatic deletion risks severing compounding backlink equity.
    \item \textbf{No Automated Canonical Tag Alterations:} Avoid accidental de-indexation of key revenue assets.
    \item \textbf{No Autonomous Overwrites on YMYL Content:} Legal, financial, and medical pages require verified expert compliance sign-off.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Economic Cost/Value ROI Simulation}
Assuming an editorial labor cost of \$150 per in-depth refresh (3 hours @ \$50/h) across a 50-URL weekly sprint (\$7,500 investment):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Model-Guided Triage (98\% P@50):} Catches 49 true decaying assets $\rightarrow$ estimated \$24,500 gross value (\textbf{+226.7\% Net Sprint ROI}).
    \item \textbf{Baseline Heuristic Triage (42\% P@50):} Catches 21 true decaying assets $\rightarrow$ estimated \$10,500 gross value (+40.0\% Net Sprint ROI).
    \item \textbf{Net Incremental Lift:} \textbf{+\$14,000 net protected value} per 50-URL editorial sprint.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Monitoring and Retrain Triggers}
We establish four explicit retrain triggers:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Google Core Algorithm Updates:} Portfolio volatility shifts by $> 2.5\sigma$.
    \item \textbf{Editorial Acceptance Drift:} Reviewer acceptance drops below 60\% over two consecutive sprints.
    \item \textbf{Covariate Feature Drift:} Traffic distribution shifts by $> 20\%$ (e.g., AI Overview referral displacement).
    \item \textbf{Quarterly Panel Refresh:} Routine 90-day retraining on fresh warehouse partitions.
\end{enumerate}
