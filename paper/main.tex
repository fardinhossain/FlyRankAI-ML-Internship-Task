\begin{abstract}
For large websites, published articles often suffer from gradual search visibility erosion and organic traffic decay on search engines over time.
Because human writing teams cannot manually check thousands of published pages each week, we study whether supervised machine learning models can accurately identify and prioritize decaying content candidates across new client websites.
To evaluate this approach, we built a leak-free dataset of 30,000 mature web pages across 32 client accounts from FlyRank's 78.8-million-row search database and evaluated candidate models using a client-grouped holdout partition where test domains were entirely isolated during training.
Under this strict evaluation on unseen clients, standalone supervised classifiers achieved top-50 precision between 0.640 and 0.780—more than doubling the 0.300 precision of heuristic baseline rules—while combining predicted decay probabilities with search demand in a composite Action Score reached a top-50 precision of 0.980.
Finally, we turned these model predictions into an easy-to-use editorial action playbook that sorts pages by potential business impact, explains why each page needs an update, and enforces clear safety rules for human writers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Website traffic that comes from Google search is one of the most valuable sources of visitors for modern online organizations. When an organization publishes helpful articles, those pages can attract steady readers for many months. However, this search visibility does not last indefinitely. Over time, competitors publish newer guides, search algorithms update their ranking preferences, and user search queries shift. As a result, older articles quietly lose their rank and traffic. This loss is especially severe near the top of Google: when an article slips from rank 4 down to rank 11, it typically loses more than 70\% of its organic clicks \cite{radlinski2010comparing, joachims2017accurately}.

This steady loss of traffic creates a serious operational challenge for content teams because writing resources are strictly limited. A large website often maintains between 10,000 and 50,000 published pages, but an editorial team can realistically review and update only 20 to 50 pages during a weekly sprint. Therefore, choosing which 50 pages to fix is a critical business decision. If editors pick the wrong pages, they waste hours rewriting articles that never recover traffic, while truly valuable pages continue to lose visitors unnoticed.

Historically, content teams have relied on simple heuristic rules to choose pages for editorial review. For example, a common practice is to flag any article older than 90 days, or any page that currently ranks on Page 2 of Google. Although these rules are easy to implement, they fail in practice for three clear reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Authority Disconnect:} A large, well-established website can hold its search rank for months without any updates, whereas a smaller website can lose its traffic in a few weeks. A fixed 90-day rule treats both websites identically, leading to poor operational guidance.
    \item \textbf{Arbitrary Cutoffs:} A rigid rule treats a page at position 20 as an urgent problem but completely ignores a page at position 21, overlooking real visitor trends, reader engagement, and trajectory.
    \item \textbf{Demand Disconnect:} A simple rule often flags an old page buried on Page 4 of Google, even though that page receives negligible searches. Updating such an inactive page wastes writer time because there are few visitors to regain.
\end{enumerate}

To address these three limitations, we test whether machine learning models can learn the observable warning signs of traffic decay and rank pages by their potential recovery value. Specifically, we investigate the following research question:
\begin{quote}
\textit{Can a supervised machine learning model, trained on observable search signals without data leakage, outperform deterministic heuristic baseline rules in ranking candidate URLs for editorial refresh on completely unseen client domains?}
\end{quote}

To answer this question, our study makes four main contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We build a clean, leak-free dataset of 30,000 mature pages across 32 client accounts from an industrial 78.8-million-row database.
    \item We show that standard random splits produce an artificial perfect score because models memorize client baselines, proving that client-grouped splits are strictly necessary.
    \item We demonstrate that supervised models more than double the triage precision of simple baseline rules on new client websites (0.640--0.780 vs. 0.300), rising to 0.980 when combined with search demand in a composite Action Score.
    \item We package these predictions into an operational editorial playbook featuring five action archetypes, explainable diagnostic reason codes, and human-review safety policies.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Search Dynamics and Position Bias}
Our work builds directly on established findings in web search and user behavior. In early information retrieval research, Radlinski and Craswell \cite{radlinski2010comparing} as well as Joachims et al. \cite{joachims2017accurately} demonstrated that user attention drops dramatically outside the top search results. Craswell et al. \cite{craswell2008experimental} modeled this drop using click cascade models, showing that searchers examine results from top to bottom and rarely scroll past the first few links. Building on these behavioral patterns, Agichtein et al. \cite{agichtein2006improving} showed that click-through rates and browsing interactions provide strong signals about whether an article satisfies user search intent. More recently, FlyRank \cite{flyrank2026whitepaper} observed that tracking longitudinal impression trends over time helps editorial teams identify decaying articles weeks before overall website traffic drops.

\subsection{Machine Learning for Tabular Ranking}
To turn these search signals into useful triage queues, researchers frequently employ learning-to-rank algorithms \cite{liu2009learning, chapelle2011yahoo}. For tabular data with skewed numeric distributions, decision tree ensembles such as Random Forests \cite{breiman2001random} and Gradient Boosted Trees \cite{chen2016xgboost} perform especially well \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}. Instead of forcing linear assumptions onto the data, tree-based models discover non-linear interactions—such as detecting when a page has substantial search volume but falling reader dwell time—without requiring complex manual feature transformations.

\subsection{Data Leakage and Honest Testing}
At the same time, applying machine learning to multi-client portfolios requires rigorous defenses against data leakage. Kaufman et al. \cite{kaufman2012leakage} showed that models often appear highly accurate in offline tests simply because features accidentally encode the target outcome. Sculley et al. \cite{sculley2015hidden} warned that hidden dependencies like these create severe technical debt in production systems. In our setting, if pages from the same client website appear in both training and testing, models memorize client-specific traffic volumes rather than general decay patterns \cite{kohavi1995study, provost1998case}. Therefore, grouping data by client account is the only fair way to evaluate how well a model transfers to new client domains. Finally, official Google documentation stresses that content should remain helpful, relevant, and up-to-date \cite{google2024helpful}, while balanced datasets avoid the need for artificial resampling methods like SMOTE \cite{chawla2002smote}.

\section{Data}
\subsection{Warehouse Source and Scale}
To conduct our experiments, we use the FlyRank AI/ML Internship Warehouse Release (\texttt{v20260703}), hosted publicly on Hugging Face at \texttt{FlyRank/internship-warehouse}. This warehouse contains 78,835,655 daily performance rows, 519,606 unique content entries, and 2,414,248 search query records across 104 client accounts over 17 months from January 27, 2025 to June 30, 2026.

\subsection{Mature Content Selection and Client Filtering}
From this large warehouse, we select only mature content items for our analysis. When an article is brand new, search engines actively test it in search results, causing temporary ranking fluctuations that do not reflect true content stability. Because of this initial volatility, we include only articles that are at least 90 days old and have received at least one search impression during the past 90 days:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_{\text{mature}} = \{ i \mid \text{imp}_{90\text{d}}(i) > 0 \;\land\; \text{age}(i) \ge 90 \}
\end{equation}
Furthermore, while the complete warehouse includes 104 client accounts, many accounts represent short tracking trials or small sites with insufficient history. To ensure robust longitudinal modeling, client accounts were included in the analytical release only if they maintained: (1) continuous 90-day search tracking telemetry without gaps; (2) a mature content inventory of at least 100 indexed URLs; and (3) verified Google Search Console telemetry. This filtering yielded a clean study cohort of \textbf{30,000 unique URLs across 32 client accounts}.

\subsection{Target Label Definition and Observation Window}
We formulate the triage task as predicting historical traffic decline. In the underlying warehouse telemetry, traffic momentum is computed by comparing impressions during the most recent 30-day window ($\text{imp}_{t-30:t}$) to impressions during the preceding 30-day window ($\text{imp}_{t-60:t-30}$):
\begin{equation}
\text{trend\_pct}_i = \frac{\text{imp}_{t-30:t}(i) - \text{imp}_{t-60:t-30}(i)}{\text{imp}_{t-60:t-30}(i)}
\end{equation}
A page is labeled as declining if its momentum drops below negative five percent ($\text{trend\_pct} < -0.05$):
\begin{equation}
y_i = \mathbb{I}(\text{trend\_direction}_i = \text{``down''})
\end{equation}
Among the 30,000 mature pages, 16,262 pages exhibit downward traffic momentum, producing a balanced natural base rate of \textbf{54.21\%}. 

\textit{Important Methodological Distinction:} We emphasize that this label represents an observed historical decline within the 90-day observation panel, rather than a forward-looking future forecast. Consequently, our system performs retrospective decline triage and candidate prioritization for human review, rather than automated future traffic forecasting.

\subsection{Data Privacy and Public Safety}
To ensure complete organizational privacy, all client account names, real URLs, and private search queries were stripped and replaced with anonymous synthetic tokens. All findings reported in this paper rely strictly on public-safe aggregate statistics.

We applied logarithmic scaling, $\log(1 + x)$, to raw traffic volumes because organic search data follows an extreme power-law distribution. A handful of top articles receive hundreds of thousands of impressions, while thousands of smaller articles receive fewer than one hundred. Logarithmic scaling prevents high-volume pages from dominating loss gradients and enables models to evaluate small and large pages on a fair scale.

\subsection{Composite Action Score Formulation}
Predicting decay probability alone is insufficient for setting editorial sprint priorities. For instance, if an article has a 95\% chance of declining but receives only five visitors per month, spending three hours to rewrite it is an inefficient use of editorial labor. To address this issue, we combine the model's predicted decay probability with search demand and ranking leverage into an operational \textit{Action Score}:
\begin{equation}
\text{Action\_Score}_i = \log(1 + \text{imp}_i) \cdot \hat{p}_i \cdot w(\text{pos}_i)
\end{equation}
Here, $\text{imp}_i$ is the 90-day impression volume, $\hat{p}_i = P(y=1 \mid X_i)$ is the model's predicted decline probability, and $w(\text{pos}_i)$ is a discrete position weight reflecting SERP click sensitivity \cite{radlinski2010comparing, craswell2008experimental}:
\begin{equation}
w(p) = \begin{cases}
1.0, & 0 < p \le 10 \text{ (Page 1)} \\
0.8, & 10 < p \le 20 \text{ (Page 2)} \\
0.5, & p > 20 \text{ (Deep SERP)} \\
0.2, & p \le 0 \text{ (Unranked)}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
These position weights are heuristic constants chosen to prioritize pages where small rank changes cause large click swings. The resulting score is linearly scaled to a 0--100 index for weekly editorial planning.

\subsection{Leakage Prevention and Auditing}
Before model training, we executed four leakage verification checks following the framework of Kaufman et al. \cite{kaufman2012leakage}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of Target Derivatives:} We stripped all columns mathematically linked to the label, including $\text{trend\_pct}$ and raw 30-day delta windows.
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of Downstream System Flags:} We eliminated existing product scores and post-outcome telemetry to avoid circular reasoning.
    \item \textbf{Intentional Leakage Sanity Check:} We conducted a deliberate leakage check by injecting the raw trend column into the training matrix. As expected, the validation score jumped to an artificial 1.0000, confirming that our pipeline detects leakage immediately. When the column was removed, the score returned to an honest 0.610.
    \item \textbf{Correlation Tower Audit:} We computed Pearson correlations between all features and the target label. The highest observed correlation was $r = 0.1901$ (active impression days), confirming that no single feature trivializes the classification task.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Setup and Validation}
\subsection{Client-Grouped Holdout Design}
To evaluate how models perform when deployed on brand-new client websites, we partitioned our data using \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit} grouped strictly on $\text{client\_id}$ with an 80/20 train/test split:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training Partition:} 23,837 pages from 25 client accounts (55.0\% decline rate).
    \item \textbf{Testing Partition:} 6,163 pages from 7 held-out client accounts (51.1\% decline rate).
    \item \textbf{Domain Isolation:} $\text{Clients}_{\text{train}} \cap \text{Clients}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset$ (zero client overlap).
\end{itemize}
Because each client account corresponds to an independent website domain portfolio, zero client overlap ensures that the model cannot memorize client-specific domain names or baseline traffic levels.

\subsection{Candidate Models and Benchmark Baseline}
We evaluated four modeling strategies on this identical test split:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline Rules (Week 4):} A deterministic heuristic formula multiplying log-impressions by discrete position and freshness tiers.
    \item \textbf{Logistic Regression:} A standardized linear classifier ($L_2$ regularization, $C=1.0$) testing monotonic relationships across normalized search signals.
    \item \textbf{Decision Tree:} A depth-constrained tree model ($\text{max\_depth}=5$) capturing non-linear step-function thresholds.
    \item \textbf{Random Forest:} An ensemble of 100 decision trees ($\text{max\_depth}=12$, $\text{random\_state}=42$) modeling complex feature interactions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Because human editorial teams can realistically review only 50 pages per week, our primary evaluation metric is \textbf{Precision@50}:
\begin{equation}
\text{Precision@}K = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i=1}^{K} y_{(i)}
\end{equation}
where $y_{(i)}$ is the true label of the candidate ranked at position $i$. Across the full test set, we also report Average Precision (PR-AUC), ROC-AUC \cite{fawcett2006introduction}, Recall, and F1-score.

\section{Discussion}
Our experimental findings indicate that organic search decay does not occur on a rigid calendar schedule. Deterministic heuristic rules assume that an article suddenly becomes obsolete at 90 days. In reality, decay is a gradual process that accelerates when an article slips to positions 11--20 while still attracting active search demand. Machine learning models succeed because they learn these smooth boundaries, avoiding the false alarms common to rigid cutoff rules.

Furthermore, our results qualify the common industry belief that article age is the dominant driver of traffic loss. In our feature analysis, content age accounts for only 7.8\% of predictive importance, whereas ranking position and impression consistency together account for over 35\%. This indicates that pages do not lose traffic merely because they are old; they decline when competitor content improves or search intent evolves. By detecting subtle ranking shifts early, machine learning enables teams to intervene before visibility completely erodes.

Analyzing model error patterns on unseen client websites highlights two primary edge cases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{False Positives:} Pages ranking near position 35 that have not been updated for months. The model identifies deep rank and high age and predicts active decline, whereas the page had in fact flatlined long ago.
    \item \textbf{False Negatives:} Highly ranked articles receiving minimal weekly impressions. Losing one impression represents a large percentage drop mathematically, but has negligible business significance. Weighting decay probability by demand resolves both issues by filtering out inactive and low-traffic pages from the priority queue.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and Honest Framing}
To preserve scientific integrity, we explicitly document the boundaries of this research:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observational Data:} Our models are trained on observational telemetry. While the models reliably identify pages exhibiting traffic decline, this association does not prove that refreshing an article will automatically restore lost rankings. Historical updates in real portfolios suffer from selection bias because human editors naturally choose to update pages they believe have high commercial potential.
    \item \textbf{No Search Engine Reverse-Engineering:} We do not claim to reverse-engineer Google's proprietary ranking algorithm. Instead, we model observable performance metrics collected across commercial tracking portfolios.
    \item \textbf{Mature Content Scope:} Our conclusions apply strictly to mature content at least 90 days old with verified search impressions, and do not describe brand-new articles.
    \item \textbf{Offline vs. Online Divergence:} High offline precision does not guarantee online traffic recovery, because real recovery depends heavily on the editorial quality and relevance of the refresh.
    \item \textbf{Platform Covariate Shift:} Search engines regularly alter SERP layouts with AI overviews and video carousels, which can change click-through rates independently of content quality.
    \item \textbf{Single Grouped Partition:} Our evaluation relies on a single 80/20 grouped holdout with 7 test clients. While domain isolation is strict, multi-split cross-validation is needed in future work to quantify client-level variance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Ranked Recommendations}
Based on our findings, we organize editorial interventions into five distinct, evidence-backed action archetypes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{refresh\_and\_review\_ctr} (24.7\% of pages):} Strong rankings (positions 1--20) but below-average click rates ($<0.5\%$). \textit{Action:} Rewrite title tags and meta descriptions to improve SERP click appeal.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{refresh} (27.7\%):} Clear decay indicators with solid underlying content. \textit{Action:} Update outdated facts, refresh statistics, and add current examples.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{refresh\_and\_review\_engagement} (7.0\%):} Steady impressions but low reader scroll depth ($<30\%$). \textit{Action:} Redesign introductory sections and improve visual readability.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{expand\_and\_refresh} ($<0.1\%$):} Visible search positions despite brief coverage ($<1,000$ words). \textit{Action:} Substantially expand content depth and topical breadth.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{monitor} (40.6\%):} Low decay probability or negligible search demand. \textit{Action:} Retain in automated monitoring without spending writer hours.
\end{enumerate}

To protect brand authority and search equity, we enforce three human-review safety policies: (1) never allow AI tools to publish refreshed articles directly without human editorial oversight; (2) never automatically delete or redirect URLs, because removing URLs can destroy accumulated backlink equity; and (3) require qualified subject-matter expert review before updating sensitive medical, legal, or financial content.

\subsection{Illustrative Economic Sprint Simulation}
To illustrate the practical utility of our system, we examine a standard weekly 50-page editorial sprint under a transparent economic simulation. Assuming an industry-standard editorial refresh cost of \$150 per in-depth revision (3 hours at \$50 per hour), reviewing 50 pages requires a dedicated budget of \$7,500. Under an operational scenario where successfully identifying and updating a true declining page protects an estimated \$500 in annual organic traffic value (assumed as 200 visits at an average search advertising equivalent of \$2.50 CPC):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Baseline Heuristic Queue (P@50 = 0.300):} Only 15 out of 50 prioritized pages represent genuine declining assets ($15 \times \$500 = \$7,500$ gross protected value). After subtracting the \$7,500 sprint cost, the net financial return is \$0 (0.0\% net ROI), meaning the baseline merely breaks even while 35 writer allocations are wasted on stable pages. (Note: in exploratory portfolio-wide sorts with alternative tie-breaking, baseline precision reached up to 0.380--0.420, yielding modest returns, but under canonical held-out test evaluation it is strictly 0.300.)
    \item \textbf{Action Score Prioritized Queue (P@50 = 0.980):} In contrast, 49 out of 50 prioritized pages represent verified historical decline candidates ($49 \times \$500 = \$24,500$ gross protected value). Deducting the \$7,500 editorial sprint cost yields an estimated net benefit of \$17,000, representing a +226.7\% net ROI.
    \item \textbf{Incremental Operational Lift:} This corresponds to an incremental net gain of +\$17,000 in protected traffic value per sprint (+34 additional true declining pages accurately targeted) compared to the heuristic baseline.
\end{itemize}
We emphasize that these figures represent an illustrative decision-support economic model based on operational cost assumptions rather than measured post-intervention revenue recovery. In our observational study, the model identifies candidates exhibiting genuine historical traffic decay; verifying actual post-refresh traffic recovery requires prospective counterfactual experimentation.

Finally, we propose retraining the model whenever search engines deploy a major core update, whenever writer agreement with triage recommendations drops below 60\%, or every 90 days as routine maintenance.

\section{Conclusion}
In this paper, we investigated how supervised machine learning can assist editorial teams in prioritizing content refresh candidates on new client websites. By analyzing 30,000 mature URLs across 32 client accounts from an industrial search warehouse, we demonstrated that supervised models achieve a top-50 precision between 0.640 and 0.780 under honest client-grouped validation, more than doubling the 0.300 precision of heuristic baseline rules. When combining predicted decay probabilities with search demand and ranking leverage in a composite Action Score, top-50 precision reached 0.980, ensuring that editorial hours target verified decaying pages.

Furthermore, our experiments showed that naive random validation causes severe domain memorization, confirming that grouping data by client account is essential for reliable evaluation. In practical application, this decision-support system transforms content maintenance from subjective guesswork into an evidence-backed editorial workflow. In future work, we plan to explore counterfactual evaluation methods to measure post-update recovery directly and develop multi-task architectures that jointly predict rank drops and click-through changes.
